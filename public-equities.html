Your framework, not a checklist

Two firms can read the same 10-K and reach opposite conclusions.

Every investor wants to buy a business for less than it's worth. Where firms differ is in how they measure that worth. A Graham-style value shop anchors on assets and normalized earnings, and wants a wide margin of safety today. A growth fund will pay up for a long runway to reinvest at high returns. A generic model can't serve both, so we don't ship one. We train the agent on your criteria, your thresholds and your past calls, for the part of the market you actually invest in.

And almost no company passes every screen. A business earning 25% on its capital usually trades well above book, and that's fair if the margins hold. So the agent weighs the factors against each other the way your analysts would. A miss lowers its confidence in the stock. It doesn't rule the stock out.

Nor is it a ratio screen. The risks that hurt a stock most tend to build quietly, across the balance sheet, the cash flows and the competitive landscape, long before they reach the headline numbers. The agent reads every filing for those slow-building problems and weighs how they could play out over the next few years. That forward view, more than any single ratio, is what sets its confidence.

Then it asks the question that matters most: does this stock make your portfolio better? Will it add to overall return, diversify what you already hold, or both?

What it does on every name.

The work a careful analyst would do, on every company you follow, each time a new filing lands.

  1. 01

    Pulls the filings

    Annual and quarterly reports, current reports and proxies come straight from SEC EDGAR as soon as they're filed. No manual downloads, no stale data.

  2. 02

    Finds real earning power

    Reported earnings are adjusted for one-off and non-recurring items such as restructuring charges, asset sale gains, write-downs and litigation, to arrive at a normalized figure the business can actually repeat.

  3. 03

    Takes the balance sheet apart

    It separates tangible from intangible assets, sets debt against total capitalization, and traces how net income flows through dividends and buybacks into retained earnings, checking that the numbers tie out.

  4. 04

    Looks for trouble years ahead

    Every line item is read for problems that haven't reached the headline numbers yet: working capital drifting out of line with sales, funding needs the business may struggle to meet, leverage building faster than equity, and a competitive position starting to erode.

  5. 05

    Judges capital allocation

    Buybacks, dividends, acquisitions and reinvestment are weighed against what they earned, including whether shares were bought back below or above what they were worth.

  6. 06

    Spots special situations

    It watches for spin-offs, restructurings, recapitalizations and bankruptcies, where forced selling, index changes and sheer complexity often push prices away from value. Each one gets the full analysis, including what the company looks like on the other side.

  7. 07

    Models the range of outcomes

    Instead of a single target price or a pass/fail screen, it builds a spread of statistical scenarios, a confidence range for how well the company fits your framework, and the probability of permanent loss, all updated as new figures come in.

  8. 08

    Places it in the portfolio

    It looks at the sector and at how the company moves with what you already hold. It estimates what the position would add to overall return and to overall risk, and whether it diversifies the portfolio or doubles up on exposure you already carry.

  9. 09

    Chooses how to own it

    The same view can be expressed in different ways. It compares outright stock, options and the company's debt, weighing payoff, downside and cost across the modeled outcomes, and suggests the structure that best fits the thesis. That might be senior debt that still recovers value in a bad scenario, or options that cap the loss.
